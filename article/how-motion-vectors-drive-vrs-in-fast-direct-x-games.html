How Motion Vectors Drive VRS in Fast DirectX Games

Variable Rate Shading (VRS) combined with motion vectors allows modern DirectX 12 engines to dramatically reduce GPU pixel workloads in fast-paced games. By analyzing the velocity of geometry across the screen, the rendering pipeline can dynamically lower shading precision on rapidly moving objects where high-frequency detail is naturally obscured by motion or blur. This article explains the technical mechanics behind motion-driven VRS, how it integrates into DirectX 12 pipelines, and how it maximizes frame rates while maintaining perceived visual fidelity.

Understanding Variable Rate Shading in DirectX 12

Variable Rate Shading is an API feature introduced in DirectX 12 that decouples pixel shader invocation rates from the render target resolution. Under standard rendering, every pixel on a 4K display executes the pixel shader once (a 1x1 shading rate). VRS allows developers to assign coarser shading rates—such as 1x2, 2x1, 2x2, or 4x4—to specific screen-space tiles.

Under VRS Tier 2, the shading rate can be determined dynamically via a screen-space shading rate image (a low-resolution texture mask). Each pixel or block in this mask specifies the shading frequency for a corresponding block of screen pixels, enabling selective allocation of GPU compute resources.

The Role of Motion Vectors

Motion vectors are 2D vectors generated during the geometry pass (G-buffer generation) that track the screen-space displacement of pixels between the current and previous frames. Typically used for temporal anti-aliasing (TAA), temporal upscaling, and post-process motion blur, motion vectors provide an exact measurement of pixel velocity.

Human visual perception cannot resolve fine spatial detail on objects traversing the display at high velocities. Because fast-moving elements are already subject to temporal smearing or deliberate motion blur passes, full-rate pixel shading on these surfaces represents wasted computational effort.

How Motion Vectors Drive the Shading Rate Mask

Integrating motion vectors into the VRS pipeline involves generating a dynamic shading rate image before the primary lighting or forward shading pass:

  1. Velocity Sampling: A compute shader samples the motion vector buffer across 8x8 or 16x16 pixel tiles, corresponding to the hardware tile size required by the GPU for VRS.
  2. Magnitude Computation: The shader calculates the velocity magnitude (speed) of the pixels within each tile. Tiles with higher average velocities are flagged for reduced shading.
  3. Thresholding and Mapping: Velocity values are mapped to supported VRS coarse rates. For example:
    • Low or zero velocity maps to full rate (1x1).
    • Moderate velocity maps to half rate (2x1 or 1x2).
    • High velocity maps to quarter rate (2x2 or 4x4).
  4. Directional Shading: Because motion vectors provide directional data, anisotropic shading rates can be applied. An object moving purely horizontally can use a 2x1 shading rate (half resolution horizontally, full resolution vertically) to preserve vertical edge sharpness while cutting horizontal shading cost in half.
  5. Mask Generation: The resulting rate assignments are written to a screen-space shading rate surface and bound to the DirectX 12 command list using RSSetShadingRateImage.

Performance Impact in High-Motion Scenarios

Fast DirectX titles—such as racing games, first-person arena shooters, and flight simulators—benefit the most from motion-driven VRS. In these titles, vast portions of the frame are in constant, rapid motion relative to the camera viewport.

By reducing the shading frequency across fast-moving backgrounds, peripheries during camera pans, and moving vehicles, the pixel shader pipeline load drops significantly. This freed GPU compute time allows for:

  • Higher overall frame rates in GPU-bound scenarios.
  • Reduced frame-time variance, leading to smoother pacing during intense action.
  • Greater power efficiency and lower thermal throttling on mobile and console hardware.

Preserving Image Quality

To prevent noticeable degradation, implementations typically apply filtering heuristics when evaluating motion vectors for VRS:

  • Edge Detection: Discontinuities in depth or normal buffers are evaluated alongside velocity to ensure that silhouette edges maintain higher shading rates, avoiding temporal aliasing.
  • Luminance and Contrast Weighting: High-contrast textures that may exhibit noticeable artifacting under coarse shading can be clamped to a finer shading rate, even at high speeds.
  • Specular Reflection Exclusions: Surfaces with sharp, dynamic specular highlights can be masked out to prevent specular flickering.

By leveraging existing motion vector data to dictate shading density, DirectX 12 titles can intelligently redistribute rendering resources, delivering substantial performance uplifts in high-speed gameplay without compromising visual clarity.
